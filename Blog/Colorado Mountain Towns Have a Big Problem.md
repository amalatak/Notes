*And it's not the one you're thinking about*

Winter Park is an awesome ski town. It's obviously got world-class skiing in the Winter, particularly in Mary Jane, but generally benefits from that high and dry Colorado powder experience. It also has a great assortment of activities in the Summer including mountain biking, hiking, summer festivals, and-much like the rest of the state-plenty of sunshine for a full, year round Mountain experience. Grading on a curve it's even more affordable than other destination towns in the area (which is probably the problem you *are* thinking about). However, like many of the other ski towns in the state it has one other problem: there's a big, fuck-off road running right through the middle of it. 

A number of ski and mountain towns in Colorado (and probably the rest of the country) share the same issue of a dual-purpose main street. At once it tries to act as a hub for the city with shopping, restaurants, and housing while at the same time serving as a state or federal highway fumigating the nearby pedestrians with exhaust gas. This huge fucking road is not only difficult to cross and dangerously close to pedestrian sidewalks, but it sonically dominates the area with conversation-killing engine sounds and general car *whooshy-ness*. The road as a main thoroughfare pushes the sidewalks to function more as an afterthought and they have little in the way of trees for shade let alone room for gardens or seating or buskers to make the street lively. Also the big road is usually hot as shit, baking passersby. 

It just generally sucks trying to enjoy a quaint mountain town experience next to a large highway, those two things are incompatible; and the pattern repeats from town to town in varying degrees from lesser offenders clearly trying to shirk this issue such Frisco, Breckenridge, and Idaho Springs to egregious victims like Leadville and Winter Park. Others include Estes Park, Idaho Springs, and Steamboat Springs. 

Before anyone gets up in arms about me being a hippie who wants communist walkable neighborhoods, which to be fair is true and I will talk about later, I'm not even trying to argue that these towns become utopia of transit (which I would love and would probably be easier than you think). For better or worse people must be met where they are and barring some *major* mandate by a *very* progressive government which I'm not optimistic will come any time soon, most people will arrive at these towns in cars, but the simple fact is that Main Street as I would hope everyone interprets it cannot be a highway. 

Which leads me to the big problem. If we are to hope for any long term vision of how these towns can recover it's to remove traffic from the highways and fill them in with sidewalks, trees, plants, seating, umbrellas, water parks, probably a thoroughfare for emergency vehicles, and much more; and based on what I mentioned earlier, the cars (for the horrible time being) will need be accommodated and to go somewhere *else*. This may be simple enough for a town like Leadville, kind of in the middle of nowhere with plenty of space to move the highway (though I'm sure even that's naive), but for a place like Steamboat Springs or Winter Park the geometry is less favorable and would likely need some form of a government doing something unpopular to a vocal few for the benefit of everyone else, and buy up their land to move a new, smaller highway through. 

I'm sure there are plenty of solutions to this that people smarter than me have put together, but at the end of the day -in towns like this- the status quo will need to be upset. Someone's stuff is going to need to get stepped on to improve the town center by running new highways, roads, or railroads somewhere *other than the downtown core*, and in towns like these usually a huge portion of the town is already built out so it's a zero-sum game, and the locals have more than enough voice and money to stave off anything bold and new. There's a reason we have a housing crisis in this state and it's not because we don't have enough timber or labor to build. That's a discussion for another day, but suffice it to say people and businesses do not like it when you change their neighborhood, regardless of the outcome. 

So where does this leave us? Truth is I really don't know. The fight against NIMBY-ism has not been nearly successful enough to give me hope.
