## The Cold War

Following WW2, there was never a general treaty such as Versailles, and the US/Soviet negotiations over territory quickly fell out. Truman thought freed countries should have free elections; Roosevelt felt that Churchill was an imperialist and was more trusting of Stalin than Truman. Stalin wanted a pro-soviet zone in Eastern Europe. He ordered the assassination of key anti-communist leaders. Churchill described an "iron curtain" descending over eastern Europe and had hoped the US would lead a western alliance. The soviets were brutally repressive of opposition in their satellite states and their economies, starved of competition and hopelessly bureaucratic and corrupt developed slower. They were also extremely polluted. Their populations resented the Soviet government and felt imprisoned. 

Britain, destroyed by the war, signaled that it could no longer maintain its imperial status and Truman filled that role, marking a global realignment. Churchill was replaced by a peacetime PM who favored relinquishing the empire. Truman (against the tradition of American isolationism), enacted the "Truman Doctrine" which pledged support for nations against authoritarian threats, indicating a direction attempting to stall the spread of communism; the policy of containment (George Kennon, long telegram) governed American policy throughout the cold war, meet the Soviet force everywhere and stop communism in its tracks. Occasionally this lead to ethical dilemmas since regimes opposing communism were themselves less than savory. 

The Marshall Plan gave money to Europe to recover quickly; this was to prevent the conditions that lead to WW2, but also to foster strong allies for defense. It was symbolized by the inequality between eastern and western Europe; though the east was invited, the soviets blocked their participation. The creation of NATO formally turned Germany into an ally. The Berlin Air lift (an air drop to ensure west Berlin supplies), cemented that alliance between west Germany and the US.

France made participation in NATO conditional on US aid in rebuilding its colonial empire in southeast Asia, which the US reluctantly accepted and would eventually regret. In 1956, Nasser government seized the Suez Canal from western companies. Britain and France wanted to retake the canal by force, but the Eisenhower administration intervened on behalf of Egypt, solidifying the US' preeminent role in global affairs.

Nuclear weapons became central to defense policy. The Soviets got the bomb in 1949 and its development in power was matched by delivery methods such as rockets and jets. Sputnik launched in the 50s. Compared to a standing army, nukes are cheap, and the US tended to demobilize and build more bombs (yay taxpayers I guess); but the soviets didn't make that choice as an autocracy (this is partly why the US never committed not to strike first). It lead to strange game theory cases around deterrence, given the destructive power of the bomb and mutually assured destruction (MAD) theory arose. In some cases, cities were targeted instead of missile silos as a sort of "hey we will only launch after you", since targeting missile silos would only work if the enemy missiles were still there. Also de-nuking for some countries obviously meant they could never strike first. 

## Second Red Scare

The Soviets got the bomb much sooner than was anticipated, leading to American fears of espionage and communist infiltration in government. It's worth noting that this particular case likely happened when the two were allies so it may not have been malicious. 

That same year, the Chinese revolution turned them communist. The country had been in civil war since 1910, but the armies of Mao and Chang Kai-shek paused hostilities to combat the Japanese invasion; this of course resumed once Japan had been defeated in WW2 and expelled. The US had been aiding Chang, but corruption and mass defections ensured his loss. Chang retrieted to Taiwan.

So Russia, China, and Eastern Europe had all rapidly become communist. It seemed unstoppable and American disloyalty was questioned. The Truman administration had begun investigating suspected communists in its ranks. Alger Hiss (high ranking American and part of the New Deal), was arrested and convicted of perjury (it seems he actually had been a spy).

Communism had been fairly popular during the depression: Capitalism had seemed to fail. But, it fell out of popularity due to Stalin's actions: he persecuted his own, the purges and kulak killings of officers and Bolsheviks, and he allied with Hitler. 

Many federal employees were fired or forced to resign under pressure. The House Committee on Unamerican Activities carried out many of these investigations (springboard of Nixon).

## Korean War

It intensified fear of communism, fearing more espionage. It tested the containment policy. The North lead by Kim Il Sung invaded South Korea in 1950. Korea had been invaded by the Japanese and then partitioned after the war under the assumption that it would be united later. Truman and Dean Atchison confirmed that they would support the South.

Americans had appeasement in mind, and were determined not to let something like that happen again. They were determined to fight enemies at the start instead of letting them get power. The UN (with the Soviets absent in protest) got a resolution to intervene (mostly lead by them). 

McArthur lead the invasion on the west coast near Seoul, pushing the North Koreans back over the 38th parallel. He pushed them so far and fast that the Chinese feared an invasion of their own and sent Chinese troops to aid the North, pushing the Americans back to just near the 38th. 

McArthur, after significant defeats, advocated total war with China to target logistic supplies and reinstall Chang (and maybe use Nukes). The US high command rejected the idea out of worry of escalation with the Soviets (also they cared less about Asia than Europe). McArthur sent a letter to Congress challenging the President, and Truman dismissed him to much controversy.

The succeeding general established a stable line, and truce negotiations began. The South Korean leader wanted a unified Korea under his leadership. It wasn't until 1953 that the truce was signed. The DMZ remains to the present. 

## McCarthyism

With the Red Scare and the Korean War, McCarthy was able to politically profit from reckless allegations of disloyalty in high places (witch hunts). For him it was a matter of convenience and not conviction; it was a strong issue, and often he would fraternize with his opposition as soon as he was done with theatrics. In 1950 he claimed Truman had knowingly failed to dismiss known communists (hinting that Truman and Democrats were treasonous communists) and he claimed to have names. 

People who aimed to dismiss McCarthy were accused of being communists themselves. He called the New Deal Era 20 years of treason. He was very shifty and theatrical, especially when the walls would close in a bit. Republicans were hesitant to call him out since they were out of power for decades and were of course the same party. 

Finally he overstepped, accusing the Army itself. Eisenhower was reluctant to take him on (his prestige probably could have stopped it sooner). In 1954 when the Army-McCarthy hearings were under way, the hearings were on television for the first time. Citizens finally saw his accusations seemed too far. 

With Eisenhower distancing himself, the people able to directly see him, the Korean war over, the strategy backfired. The Senate censured McCarthy. This all demonstrated how US life was so preoccupied by the fear of Communism. 

## Domestic Issues at the Time

WW2 had contributed heavily to economic prosperity with high wages, full employment, and generous VA benefits. Americans had at first feared the depression might resume. But now poverty became more rare and people questioned why it existed (instead of "why are people wealthy"). The G.I. Bill had enabled veterans to gain low cost education and favorable mortgage terms. This caused a boom in college attendance. 

Suburbs popped up everywhere with standardized and prefabricated parts (some people saw the homes as soulless). The country sprawled, especially to the sun belt (LA to Miami), growing around the automobile with wide spread out areas absent dense downtowns (A/C also enabled this more). The strip mall was born. The baby boom ensued aided by improvements in medicine (low infant mortality and longer life expectancy).

Marketing targeted more specific demographics (Disneyland for children). Las Vegas was created, and rabid fans of Sinatra (and soon Presley) began. Cars became commonplace, the highway was built stimulating the economy (also with some defense in mind). Airlines gradually replaced trains. High incomes allowed for the single-income home with mothers as homemakers. 

Anxieties of nuclear war tempered optimism. People built their own fallout shelters (fun ethical questions: do I let in my desperate neighbor) and kids practiced bomb drills in school. Intellectuals were concerned about conformity and the decline of individualism (countercultures would later react to this). Anxiety around scarcity was replaced by existential anxiety of purpose. Advertising began shaping American ideas, with more money spent on ads than on churches or schools. Society worried about juvenile delinquency. The USSR surprised space race lead to an American dedication to education and technology. American culture enjoyed worldwide prestige instead of Europe, and NYC became the capital of the art world. Skyscrapers dominated cityscapes and large corporations dominated consumerism. Universities boomed. 

The affluent society: poor people know what their problems are, but the wealthy can find a number of issues to be concerned about. The book raised concerns that this money may not be spent well.
