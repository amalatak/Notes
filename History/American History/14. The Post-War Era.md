## Civil Rights Movement

Brown vs. Board of Education (of Topeka KS) in 1954 was the culmination of the struggle of desegregation and was lead by the NAACP, decided schools must be desegregated and overturning Plessy v. Ferguson (separate was inherently not equal, despite southern attempts to build good schools for segregated people). A following decision called Brown 2 said desegregation should happen with all due speed. Southerners dragged their feet as much as possible.

The military (and federal hiring) abandoned segregation after WW2 under Truman. Baseball was desegregated. Civil rights split the Democratic party on North/South divide and southerner segregationists ran a Dixiecrat. Wallace progressives also left as less antagonistic of the USSR. It was thought Truman would lose but he won. This showed that elections could be won with civil rights.

MLK Jr became famous for the Montgomery bus boycott. The buses were segregated and Rosa Parks agreed to challenge the laws by sitting in an area for white passenger. MLK was appointed to lead the protest. King originally feared people would abandon buses but they didn't He maintained solidarity through church prayer services. King had a lot of death threats. He engaged in peaceful persuasion instead of coercion. It succeeded. 

Civil rights leaders chose successive protests to be where they knew segregation opposition would be to generate news. Sit ins were in NC to desegregate stores, freedom rides on buses. NAACP would call the press to witness white violence. The black members trained how to encounter racist violence without fighting back, borrowing insights from Gandhi. King leveraged the shared language of the Bible as well. In Atlanta, the mayor put forth a desegregation plan peacefully to promote its own image. 

The JFK administration dragged its feet on desegregation, knowing they needed the white southern vote (MLK didn't like JFK very much). 

In Birmingham, AL, King staged a protest knowing the Commissioner of Public Safety was a racist segregationist die-hard and would overreact. This worked. While King was in jail from this protest, he wrote a very famous letter highlighting the movements global importance. Later that year, segregationists bombed some churches, killing some young girls. This caused Bobby Kennedy to finally jump into action (especially against states rights) and later King gave his Dream speech, and the next year he won the Nobel Peace Prize.

All the while, the Soviets published whites attacking blacks in the South as propaganda for themselves. At the same time, Europe was decolonizing and the US and Soviets were competing for African interests. So the federal government recognized the poor image some of this gave the US in that global struggle for African attention in the propaganda war.

An alternative offered by the Black Muslim movement (Malcom X). The Nation of Islam offered a mirror image of white racism. Eliza Mahammad preached whites came from a strange genetic experiment as white devils to subjugate blacks. It was an effective movement in restoring dignity to degraded blacks. They made you live a clean, well dressed, hard working life believing in black assertion. Malcom X advocated for black separatism and criticized King. He struck fear in white communities, making King more appealing. Malcom X was assassinated after moving away from the Nation of Islam.

Legally sanctioned racism was destroyed quickly, and most southerners caved with little issue. Sometimes they did "token" integration, accepting only a few of the very best performing blacks. Congress changed this: Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act (64, 65) and the immigration act of 1965 ended racial discrimination in immigration policy. In the 60s, riots broke out in the South, destroying the "good feelings" of the civil rights movements. The cause is not certain, but it may have been caused by blacks seeing how poorly off they were despite legally equal footing. 

Soon after, affirmative action was implemented to discriminate in black favor, leading to white backlash. White resistance increased, attacking integration buses and white flight residential patterns moved whites away from integrated areas. Now, the black middle class moved away from cities with white flight making things very bad in cities. Black Americans remained underprivileged even compared to new immigrants. 

## The Great Society

Kennedy's father was a bootlegger and investor who promoted his sons. JFK was elected to congress and then the senate. Jackie put up with infidelity and the press still didn't show it. JFK was sickly. 

His Catholicism was uncertain (people thought he would be subservient to the Pope), but he delivered a speech defining his separation between Church and State, that he would resign if there was a conflict of interest and that it was true of all the rest of them (he also didn't think he was a good Catholic). In '62, the Second Vatican Council redefined their principles from regarding protestants as heretics to be more like their separated brethren. The election of JFK and the Vatican Council's actions softened national stance on Catholicism

He very narrowly defeated Nixon, aided by TV (radio/TV debate). JFK pursued the Cold War more aggressively to close the missile gap and he thought Eisenhower was too passive in accepting the Cold War as a "stalemate". He wanted to engage more actively especially in Vietnam. As he came into office, Castro (aligned with USSR) displaced Batista in Cuba and they planned an invasion of exiles (the Bay of Pigs) was backed by the US (not using air support or direct military support) but was overwhelmed; this was terrible PR for the US and Kennedy. Cuba was given aid by Khrushchev, who sent missiles to Cuba. Kennedy sent the Navy with a quarantine line that the Soviet ships would not go past. Khrushchev backed down and the US withdrew its own missiles from Turkey. In '63 the atmospheric test ban was signed. 

JFK did limited initiatives for woman and minorities and the poor, but his small victory and dependence on the South hampered him. He did raise minimum wage, crated a commission for women, and some affordable housing stuff. Bobby was more sympathetic about civil rights, sending federal troops to 'ol Miss. 

Of course, we will never know how this would end. JFK was assassinated in Nov, 1963 and Oswald was shot soon after. JFK was turned larger than life, but his accomplishments were limited and his foreign policy was somewhat reckless. LBJ was sworn in on the plane.

LBJ came from a Texas farm and set about to finish JFK's policies. He was part of the New Deal and a political insider. He was the Senate Majority Leader when he was asked to be VP. He and JFK didn't get along and there was constant friction between him and JFK aids. He passed a lot of legislation: civil rights act, voting rights act, immigration reform act. Barry Goldwater ran to undo basically every New Deal policy (Social Security, abolish income tax) and was quite tactless; and LBJ crushed him. 

LBJ continued the war on poverty. There was a book on "The Other America", that the poor were left out. A competing theory was "the culture of poverty", that the poor kept themselves poor: incapable of saving or thinking long term. LBJ created a bunch of programs to address this. They also created Medicare and Medicaid. 

The inner city race riots severely undercut the good feelings of the the LBJ administration and the utopian goals of eliminating poverty. Also more and more political capital was donating to Vietnam. 

## Mass Media

Mass circulation of newspapers accompanied mass literacy in the early 20th century, and public schooling assured rapid assimilation of immigrants. Early in the century, there was a massive foreign-language press. The newspaper provided civic identity for a given city as they grew. Pulitzer and Randolph-Hurst understood how modern media would work through headlines, pictures, low prices, cartoon strips, and good writing. Yellow journalism was a term for these early papers. Early pictures (before photography) were painted.

They loved melodrama as well. They were responsible for whipping up a narrative that encouraged US to go to war against Spain in 1898, alleging a Spanish plot to blow up the USS Maine. They also engaged in Muckraking, anti-trust publishing (but moderating when necessary).

Radios made it possible to send morse code wirelessly and the audion tube made it possible to carry audio. In 1917 the Navy used radio and in 1920 civilian broadcasting began. Evangelical preachers bought in very quickly. Ads were used for revenue instead of licensing fees like in the UK. The FCC was eventually created to regulate the use of the air waves. Ownership of radios exploded over the 20s and 30s.

Politicians quickly understood the power of this (e.g. fireside chats), and negative campaigning was used. Fiction was carried on the medium. Radio journalism became extremely important, e.g. shaping American attitudes as pro-British in early WW2. In 1938, a rendition of War of the Worlds convinced many people that it was real. 

Television intensified the affects from radio. It was slow to gain traction due to WW2 but grew meteorically after that. TV game show scandals (21) led to laws against abuses of the medium. It helped bring down McCarthyism by showing Americans his brazen, false attacks; it's importance was also captured in the Nixon-JFK debates. Nixon actually thought he would do well due to his prior experience in radio with the Checkers affair. After his eventual election his aids always made sure to pretty him up and be ready for the screens. 

The Army had hoped to gain the favor of public opinion by allowing televisions in Vietnam, but it backfired, showing the horrors of war. MLK used it widely, and the moon landing was televised. In 1991, television was more informing than the government in the Baghdad raid.

The transistor, silicone chip, and rapid advancements in computers led to the internet and the information age playing out today, its affects are still being determined but its thought to drive more division than bring people together. 

## Vietnam

Vietnam was a French colony in the early 20th century. Japan invaded in 1942 but was ousted by the allies after the war. Ho Chi Minh declared independence based on the American model but the French, aided by the Americans for a quid pro quo for participation in NATO, returned (this made Americans very uncomfortable since they were typically anti-colony). HCM and his Vietminh ousted them at Dian Bien Phu in 1954 and the French empire in Vietnam ended in 1955. In WW2 Asian powers had learned they could defeat western powers (Japan ousting France). Geneva accords ended French empire in Vietnam, split the country in two, and said that after a period of time elections would take place. 

America was determined to defend Vietnam as part of containment and to avoid another Munich mistake of appeasement; there was also Domino Theory that one country falling would cause another. The US then underwrote the south Vietnam non-communists preventing elections from taking place to reunify. The Americans offered to support the southern leader, General Diem, to avoid elections that would have gone for HCM, uniting the country as communist. However, Diem was so autocratic and corrupt he never developed legitimacy and the Vietcong developed as an internal guerilla group in the south against his government. The various opponent groups coalesced into the National Liberation Front (NLF) to unify Vietnam as a communist country. 

In the 50s, Americans were still idealist about Vietnam. They sought to act as good Christian aids. But Diem's deep unpopularity made it difficult. They were parts of the Catholic minority ruling very heavily. Some Buddhist monks set themselves on fire in protest. Diem (the US knew would happen) was assassinated and then the country shuffled through a bunch of deeply unpopular southern leaders, dependent on American aid. 

Johnson used an incident in the Gulf of Tonkin to stimulate American response. The Senate authorized Johnson to use force. Americans didn't want to use too much force to avoid Russian or Chinese intervention and the military disliked the holding action. Americans ended up controlling areas during the day and then the Vietcong would move in at night, putting locals in a weird place. The terrain made it difficult and they relied on helicopters. They could never win the allegiance of the south Vietnamese people (heart and minds).

New Journalism developed in the war with more dramatic editorializing. The US committed atrocities such as the Meline Massacre, in part due to not knowing if the civilians were friendly or enemies. Morale dropped and soldiers would kill overzealous officers (fragging); drug abuse was widespread. Casualties and footage of tragedies turned Americans against the war in universities and churches. Young men deserted or dodged the draft. Student deferments meant that a person could defer a draft by going to college, adding a socioeconomic aspect to this as the poor couldn't afford college. For the first time ever, it was socially acceptable to be against the war. There was a huge generation gap between WW2 vets and their sons. The army was denied overall victory. People protested against the abstract goals. MLK spoke against the war. 

LBJ dropped out of the race in 1968. The Tet Offensive sealed his fate. Generals thought there was a light at the end of the tunnel, but during the offensive the embassy in Saigon was under attack, making clear the war was nowhere near over (even though it was a tactical defeat). The democratic candidates split over war policy. Bobby entered the race and was assassinated, MLK was assassinated. 

Nixon won the Republican nomination running on the "silent majority", people who supported fighting the war to an honorable end; not the violently passionate. Peace talks began in Paris. He widened the war into Cambodia drawing more protests (an incident happened at Kent state in Ohio).

The war finally ended in 1973 after a long draw down. The US was not able to return to prevent the overtake after they left in part due to Watergate. The brutal oppression of the communists after the war, and the genocide in Cambodia led some Americans to reflect on the war that the US had been justified. In the end, there were a few effects on future wars: the Army was to be conservative and cautious where it went to war, would restrict media access and manipulate releases, and there must be an exit strategy. 

## Women's Movement

Middle class women during the 50s and 60s were bored confined to housework. They were extremely easy living (better food, better medicine, appliances, enthusiasm for the future), but they felt they were wasting their lives, especially those with degrees. Many of them turned to drugs (tranquilizers). The baby boom wasn't enough fulfillment. 

Many women wanted a different purpose, and with longer life expectancy women who married young could have a lot of time even after the kids went off to school. At this time, discrimination against women was legal: they could be denied jobs and paid lower. 

Many lower class women had been working this entire time due to economic necessity. Women's work was less well paid and that tended to contribute to their financial suffering. A Kennedy commission found that women were discriminated against in jobs, payment, and law. 

In the 60s the women's movement began. National Organization of Woman (NOW) campaigned against gender discrimination in employment and congress did pass laws stipulating equal pay and opportunity. This led to a sharp rise of women in professional, high profile jobs. Early on there was "token" women in companies but that declined by the 80s. Access to childcare became a central debate: what to do with them and who is responsible (govt vs. private and men vs. women). 

Women protested against social discrimination and expectations (sexism). Consciousness raising was a process to tell women they were being victimized. They denounced beauty contests. They conducted demonstrations: 1970 NY demonstration. 

They protested the mrs. vs. miss. The glass ceiling. A cult of personality grew around Gloria Steinman (she was pretty so newspapers asked her for the "women's opinion"). Radical feminists saw the world as a conflict between men and women and that rape is a tool of subjugation to keep women afraid. Lesbianism was debated as a method of removing men from life.

Meanwhile, the sexual revolution was under way, enabled by the pill. In the 60s people thought the world population would explode so the pill was socially benign. The Kinsey report on male and female sexuality (48, 53) found high levels of premarital, extramarital, and same sex activities. Hugh Hefner premiered playboy. Sex and the single girl was published (soon the same woman began Cosmopolitan). In '68, the Catholic Church shook itself when the Pope announced sex was only for reproduction. 

The taboo of cohabitation and child rearing out of wedlock was dropped, as it was for abortion. Originally, anti-abortion laws were written by Catholics and then Protestants in an attempt to prevent Catholic births from overtaking protestant populations. Sherry Finkbine case covered therapeutic abortion and people cried out against her abortion calling it infanticide, which brought the issue into the spotlight. Radical feminists campaigned for radical freedom to do what they wanted with their bodies and this was codified in Roe v. Wade (overturned).

The gay liberation movement began in 1969 with many of the same goals. The stonewall riot, patrons of a gay bar stayed to fight against a police raid. Coming out became a central issue to gays. Until 1973 it had been considered a disability whereas now homophobia is. The AIDs epidemic intensified debates around homosexuality and drug use. 

Inevitably all of this change led to a backlash. States de-ratified the ERA (not sure if that's doable), the anti-abortion movement began. In the end, an increase in workforce participation from women drove up supply of labor such that now many women must work out of necessity. 

## Watergate

Nixon became famous from the Alger Hiss trial, he later became VP to Ike as the political guy (Eisenhower had no experience) and he became a star from the checkers apology. He was elected in 1968, noting that the solid south was breaking up especially in the wake of the civil rights movement and affirmative action. He appealed to the "silent majority". George Wallace was running as third party "law and order", he heavily criticized pot-smokers, welfare cheats, long hair, draft dodgers but Nixon still won. 

Nixon opened diplomatic relations with China, the first to do so since it became Communist; Kissinger conducted a series of secret trips then Nixon went (Carter completed the full relations). This allowed Nixon to leverage the intra-communist tension with the Russians, but he also believed in keeping the cold war cold. Completed the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT), and he went to Moscow where both sides agreed to reduce nuclear arms build up as well as the ballistic missile treaty where they agreed not to build defensive missiles (can be interpreted as offensive). He also pulled out of Vietnam and abolished the draft, but his handling of the drawdown (slow, Kent State Massacre, invasion of Cambodia) was poor so he remained unpopular with the left. Saigon fell and the South was completely defeated. 

Domestically, he accepted Johnson's Great Society legacy (both houses were democrat). School desegregation continued. He disliked media. 

The Watergate Scandal ruined him. It was totally unnecessary; he had presided over the moon landing, was overseeing detente with the Russians. A group of burglars (plumbers) bugged the DNC headquarters but they were caught and denied link to the White House, but two WaPo journalists heard about it and pursued the story (after lobbying their editors). Deepthroat (an informant) helped them. Nixon won in 1972 easily. He had to navigate appearing to persecute the investigation while covering it up. Eventually investigators and journalists uncovered evidence that Nixon and his aides engaged in burglary, bribery, spying (on sex lives), blackmail, destruction of evidence, perjury, and pardons of quiet people. The question was how much did Nixon know, and the breakthrough came when they discovered Nixon had tape recordings of discussions in the Oval Office. The Senate asked for them, Nixon refused claiming executive privilege. Eventually he was forced to turn them over and they incriminated them. Additionally, transcripts were full of bad language and slurs (expletive, deleted). 

It's not obvious why Nixon didn't just destroy the tapes early on, but he hoped to use them as justification for history and there was a long history of spying on ones enemies. FDR, JFK had done it. Why he did the bugging on the DNC isn't obvious either. 

In 1974, the house was going to indict him and congress denied him approval for renewed efforts in Vietnam (the North had violated a treaty by invading). Watergate had dominated political workings for two years. Finally, facing impeachment and conviction, he resigned, denying it to the end. Ford took over since Spero Agnew (VP) had also resigned. 

Vietnam and Watergate created a sour mood towards government and by the mid 70s even average Americans were losing trust in their Government officials. 
## Environmentalism

The movement began in the 60s as a response to pollution and population. The sky in some cities were literally smoggy (sometimes seen as a sense of pride for work), but by the mid century, medicine had shown this was linked to health problems. Carson published a book on indiscriminate pesticides and herbicides, particularly DDT (a miracle pesticide at the time) which didn't decompose at the time and biomagnified. This ruined trust in American chemical manufacturers. Also consumer advocacy became a thing (Ralph Nader w/ Cars and GM had investigators follow him which Nader sued and won to setup Nader's Raiders). Finally the population growth fears led to this, people thought growth was unlimited. The Sierra Club campaigned to end damming of rivers. 

The first Earth day was celebrated in 1970. Ecological activism ran the whole gamut (preservationists to eco-terrorists). It was mostly appealing to upper-middle class white people, since they usually have all of their primary needs accounted for to worry about the environment. Legislation gave protection to the environment: the clean water and air acts cut smog standards and acid rain, leaded gas was banned, a river in Cleveland caught fire which disgraced the city and country. 

Only wealthy societies can afford environmental regulations, since they raise prices in theory. The national environmental policy act created the EPA stating that environmental impacts needed to be studied for construction projects. Enhanced protection for parks and wildlife were passed (endangered species act)- sometimes used aggressively to prevent projects. 

The energy crisis of the 70s tested the nations dedication to environmentalism, particularly the oil crisis which led to the construction of the trans-Alaska pipeline. The political volatility of the middle east. Israel had been at war with Arab neighbors since its creation in 1948, with flash points in 1956 for the Suez canal crisis, in 1967 with the 6-day war, and in 1973 with the Yom-Kippur war. American policy, driven by the pro-Israel lobby, had been highly favorable to Israel, but the more the US sided with Israel, the more Arab neighbors wanted to retaliate and in 1973 exports were cut down. There was an oil spill related to it on a boat.

Nuclear power utilities were aided by the government to build out their infrastructure. It was safer than coal mining and didn't need to flood valleys with dams. Questions around the used fuel rods, some scandals led to doubts in the 70s and then the 3-mile island accident (no deaths) led to mass protests and a belief that it was too dangerous, even though it's much safer than any other energy form. 

Environmentalism was very popular, even during the Reagan era when his secretary of the interior believed development was far more important than conservation. His unpopularity led to the growth in environmental groups and he was eventually canned. 

## Religion in Modern America

After the first world war, protestant churches became more fractious and they split over historicity of the Bible and its meaning and evolution (evangelicals/fundamentalists vs liberal). See Scopes Monkey Trial (noted earlier) where fundamentalists were seen as radical until the 80s/90s. Reinhold Nyberg advocated for imperfection in people and for war against Germany and fighting communism. 

Catholics functioned to assimilate different groups by remaining unified across groups. They also had built a school system. The Legion of Decency played a role in Hollywood, angered by the depiction of sex, drugs, and gangster films. Hollywood setup the Hayes Office to self-police movies to present issues acceptably. 

Jews were a small but influential group and they split over assimilation, some remaining highly orthodox. An American invention is the conservative Jew which struck a middle ground.

Religious tolerance increased in the second half of the 20th century. The Holocaust and Israel ended American antisemitism. Early on, there were melting pots within religions and now those religious barriers are breaking down. Kennedy's election in 1960 and the Second Vatican Council contributed to the end of American anti-Catholicism (people had thought that their loyalty was to the Pope). 

In addition, SCOTUS rulings increased separation: in 1962, ruled that school children couldn't start the day with a prayer; in 1963, ruled that there can't be Bible reading in schools. This violated a tradition of protestant worship in public schools. This enraged many church leaders and people tried to impeach Warren. Amendments to restore God to the classroom always failed and evangelical private academies cropped up in response. 

The 1960s saw new movements such as the Jesus Movement (Jesus Freaks). The Hippies rejected mainstream society (were kind of Buddhist, love not ware, good feelings, etc) and felt that Jesus was like them. The summer of 1967, summer of love. 

Also the People's Temple; Jim Jones was an evangelical minister and created a color-blind (mostly poor) following. He was convinced a nuclear war was going to happen and moved the community from Indianapolis to California. He came to think of himself as divine. He was originally popular, he was able to get people off drugs. Others thought he was a brainwasher, in response he moved his settlement to Guyana. A congressman went to check on Jonestown to make sure no one was being held against their will, but as he left he was ambushed and killed. After that, Jones made everyone commit mass suicide (Kool Aid). After that, the deprogramming movement began to remove people from religious cults and any new movement lived in the shadow of Jonestown. 

In the 1970s women began to question their roles and some protestant churches began ordaining women soon after, as did reform Jews. Catholics refused. Some interpret the Bible as more feminist. Disenchanted Americans with Judeo-Christian dominance turned to Asian alternatives such as Zen Buddhism, transcendentalism (Beetles). 

New immigrants further diversified this (the US ended National Origin distinctions). Islamic populations climbed from blacks, particularly in the Nation of Islam, Indians, Iranians, Pakistanis, Yemenis, etc, brought in Sunni Islam. This (particularly Islam worship many times a day) raised questions about how to accommodate the free exercise of religion. Political tensions after 1980 led to Islamic discrimination (and sometimes attacks), during the Iranian Revolution, the gulf war, and after Sept. 11. 

Hindus entered the country in large numbers, and found it hard to not change their life during integration: they changed rituals to try and keep Hindu culture. Hispanic Catholics immigrated in mass. The US remains unusual for its religious vitality and diversity, while evangelicals staked returns dismayed by moral revolutions of the 60s/70s.
